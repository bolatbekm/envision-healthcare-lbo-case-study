# The Deal: What Happened, Year by Year

*(All figures cited in `sources.md`.)*

## Before 2018: a company with a thin and shrinking margin

Envision Healthcare Corporation was formed in December 2016 by a "merger
of equals" between Envision Healthcare Holdings and AmSurg Corp. Its own
FY2017 10-K shows operating margin falling sharply — **24.1% (FY2015) →
10.6% (FY2016) → 2.4% (FY2017)** — though part of that decline reflects
the 2016 merger changing the size and shape of the company, not pure
organic deterioration. Either way, on $7.8 billion of FY2017 revenue,
operating income was just $189 million. The Physician Services segment
specifically posted an operating *loss* of $221 million that year. This
was not a company with much margin for error.

It's also worth knowing this wasn't Envision's first time through private
equity: its Physician Services predecessor, EmCare, had already been taken
private once before — by Clayton, Dubilier & Rice in 2011, for about $3
billion — before going public again in 2013 and merging with AmSurg in
2016.

## 2018: KKR buys in

In June 2018, Envision agreed to be acquired by KKR for **$46.00 per
share** in cash, a 32% premium, valuing the deal at **~$9.9 billion**. KKR
committed **$3.5 billion of equity**, backed by debt financing commitments
of **up to $8.05 billion** from a lender syndicate including Credit
Suisse, Citigroup, Morgan Stanley, Barclays, and Goldman Sachs. The deal
closed October 11, 2018. Envision issued $1.225 billion of public Senior
Notes the same day, then **deregistered from SEC reporting eleven days
later** — it would file no further annual reports.

## 2020-2021: COVID, and the first round of "extend and pretend"

The pandemic hit Envision's business model — physician staffing and
outpatient surgery — directly. The company's own later accounting:
**~$1.1 billion of lost revenue and ~$415 million of lost EBITDA in
2020**, plus a further **~$380 million revenue impact in 2021**. Against
an already-thin pre-deal margin and a freshly-leveraged balance sheet,
this was a serious blow.

Rather than restructure immediately, the company (through its AMSURG
subsidiary) executed a **debt-exchange transaction**: raising **$1.1
billion of new-money first-lien debt** and exchanging **~$2.0 billion** of
existing debt for **~$1.4 billion** of new AMSURG second-lien debt —
capturing **~$600 million** of "discount" (creditors accepted less than
face value, reducing the debt balance on paper) in exchange for extended
maturities. This is the kind of transaction sometimes called "extend and
pretend" in distressed-debt circles — it buys time without fixing the
underlying earnings problem.

## 2018-2022: a specific payor dispute

Separately from COVID, Envision identified a specific health insurer
(referenced elsewhere in the same court filing as UnitedHealthcare) as an
"outlier" whose behavior cost the company **more than $400 million in
cumulative revenue** through several specific tactics: unilaterally
changing contract terms (~$100M, 2018), extracting additional rate cuts
(~$100M), denying payments at a rate of ~$50M/year since 2021, and
underpaying on claims it did pay by more than $200M. Envision eventually
won a **$91 million arbitration award** against the payor over this
conduct — vindication, but not a cash recovery that solved the structural
problem. On top of this, general inflation added **~$330 million** of cost
versus 2019 spending levels.

## 2022: round two

In 2022, Envision ran a second liability-management exercise: **$300
million** of new-money "first out" term loans, and the conversion of
**$3.7 billion** of existing term loans into "second out" and "third out"
tranches — capturing **more than $1 billion** of additional discount and
extending maturities on over 97% of the relevant debt.

Combined across both rounds, Envision's creditors had agreed to accept
**over $1.6 billion less than face value** on paper. It wasn't enough.

## May 2023: Chapter 11

Envision Healthcare Corporation filed Chapter 11 on **May 15, 2023**, in
the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the Southern District of Texas (Houston). At
filing: **$655 million** of cash and **$7.7 billion** of funded debt,
spread across a **"dual-silo" structure** — the EHC/Physician Services
financing stack and the AMSURG financing stack, loosely connected by
intercompany loans (~$983M short-term, ~$848M long-term outstanding).

**KKR had not left.** Unlike Golden Gate Capital at Red Lobster, the
"Consenting Sponsors" still held **99.67% of the equity** in the parent
holding company at the moment of filing. More than that: KKR had **bought
~$146 million of Envision's own distressed "second out"/"third out" Term
Loans and ~$253 million of its Senior Notes** — putting additional money
into the company's debt stack even as the equity position it already held
was heading toward zero.

## October-November 2023: the outcome

Both the EHC and AMSURG restructuring plans were confirmed on **October
11, 2023**, and the reorganized companies emerged on **November 3,
2023** — 172 days after filing. Roughly **70% of prepetition funded debt
was eliminated**. Envision split permanently into two independent
companies — **AmSurg** and **Envision Physician Services (EVPS)** — each
with new ownership, as former senior creditors (led by **PIMCO**, with
**Blackstone** and **Brigade Capital** among others) converted their debt
holdings into equity and became the new controlling owners.

**What happened to KKR's $3.5 billion equity commitment is not fully
settled in the sources reviewed for this project.** One report states KKR
retained a minority stake in the reorganized AmSurg; another states KKR
retains no ownership stake in either successor company. Both are presented
in this project's sources — what both agree on is that KKR's *controlling*
equity position, and the overwhelming majority of its value, did not
survive.

## What this project argues

Three case studies, three different patterns of how a sponsor relates to
the collapse it's connected to:

- **Toys "R" Us**: the sponsor holds conventional debt for over a decade
  and is there when it breaks.
- **Red Lobster**: the sponsor engineers a structural problem (the
  sale-leaseback) and leaves years before it detonates under different
  owners.
- **Envision Healthcare**: the sponsor never leaves, and even adds to its
  position in the company's distressed debt on the way down — the
  collapse here wasn't one bad decision or one missed exit, it was a
  thin-margin company absorbing COVID, a specific adversarial payor
  relationship, inflation, and two rounds of debt restructuring that
  bought time without buying a solution.
